There's a particular kind of tension that settles over a room when smart people are describing a problem they can all see but none of them have fully solved.

I felt it last week in Sydney.

I was lucky enough to sit around a table with twelve senior technology leaders, CIOs, CTOs, and other executives responsible for the technology direction of some of Australia's largest organisations. Good food, good conversation, and an open brief to talk honestly about where they actually are with artificial intelligence. Not where the press releases say they are. Not where their CEOs want them to be. Where they actually are.

By the end of lunch, one delegate had shared a single statistic that reframed everything else we'd discussed. But to understand why it mattered so much, you need to understand what the rest of the room was dealing with.

What followed was one of the most grounding conversations I've had in a long time. And it surfaced something I think the broader technology industry needs to hear.

Let's set the scene properly, because context matters here.

These weren't small businesses experimenting with AI on the side. These were large enterprises, operating across a broad range of industries. They have the budgets. They have the mandates. Several of them have direct pressure from their CEOs and boards to move faster on artificial intelligence. They've read the same reports the rest of us have about productivity gains and competitive advantage and the Kodak moment that awaits those who move too slowly.

And almost none of them are seeing meaningful value from AI. Not yet.

That's not a criticism. It's an observation. And more importantly, it's a clue.

## The Wrong Diagnosis

When AI projects fail to deliver, the instinct, especially in the media, is to blame the technology. Hallucinations. Model limitations. Integration complexity. The gap between the demo and the deployment.

Those things are real. But they're not what came up around this table.

What came up, again and again, in different words from different organisations across wildly different industries, was something far more fundamental.

Data. Not the AI. The data underneath it.

One delegate put it plainly. 

> "We need to get our data right first. That's the foundation for everything."

Another said they were holding off on any meaningful AI rollout until they had resolved their data ownership problem. Another was mid-project trying to classify and understand what data they even had before they could think about what to do with it. Another had spent two and a half years methodically cleaning their data estate, deleting millions of records and assigning executive-level data owners, and still felt they were only partway there.

The pattern was impossible to ignore. The bottleneck isn't the AI. It's the trust, or lack of it, in the data the AI would be built on.

## What Data Trust Actually Means

Trust is a word that gets thrown around a lot in technology. But in this context it has a very specific meaning, and it has several distinct dimensions that the conversation kept returning to.

**Can you see your data?**

Visibility was the first and most common concern. Multiple leaders around the table couldn't confidently answer basic questions about their own data estate. What data do we have? Where does it live across our infrastructure? Who has access to it? What's being generated by the AI tools our people are already using?

That last question opened up a significant thread around shadow AI, where employees use personal or unsanctioned AI tools with company data, entirely outside the organisation's view. Several delegates admitted it was happening in their organisations right now, and they had limited ability to see it, let alone control it. One was deploying tooling specifically to get visibility across AI usage in their environment. Another was considering a different approach altogether. If people are using shadow AI because the approved tools aren't good enough, make the approved tools better.

But the underlying issue is the same. You cannot trust what you cannot see. And if you can't see your data, where it is, how it's being used, what it's being fed into, you have no foundation for trusting anything that AI produces from it.

**Who owns your data?**

Ownership came up at almost every turn, and the contrast between delegates was stark.

One organisation had done the hard work of assigning real accountability across their data estate, with specific people responsible for the accuracy and appropriateness of specific data domains at a senior level. But what made it genuinely stand out was that this wasn't just an IT initiative quietly running in the background. The progress of that data cleanup was visible at board level. System owners could see where they stood, and leadership could see it too. Data hygiene had been elevated to a business performance metric, tracked and reported with the same rigour as any other organisational priority. It was unglamorous work. It took time. But it meant that when data needed to be trusted, there was someone whose job it was to ensure that trust was warranted, and an entire leadership chain that could see whether they were doing it.

Most of the table hadn't gotten there. Data ownership in many large organisations is still murky. Data exists, but nobody is specifically accountable for whether it's accurate, current, or appropriate to use. In that environment, feeding data into an AI system isn't a technical decision. It's a gamble.

Data without an owner is data nobody is responsible for. And AI built on unowned data will reflect that irresponsibility in its outputs.

**How clean is your data?**

The oldest principle in data, garbage in, garbage out, hasn't changed. But the stakes have.

Several delegates described data estates with significant sprawl. Years of accumulated records, unstructured data with no clear classification, duplicates, outdated information, and data that had long outlived any legitimate purpose but had never been deleted because nobody was sure it was safe to do so. One delegate described it simply as data rot (the gradual degradation of data quality over time, as records become outdated, duplicated, or simply abandoned with no owner to maintain them).

The danger this poses to AI is subtle and therefore more serious than most organisations appreciate. When you feed stale or inaccurate data into a large language model or a retrieval system, you don't always get obviously wrong outputs. You get *confidently wrong* outputs. The model sounds authoritative. The answer looks clean. But the foundation beneath it is compromised. And in an enterprise context where decisions about customers, risk, compliance, and strategy are being made, that's not an acceptable margin of error.

**Can you recover if something goes wrong?**

This dimension of data trust is the one I think is most underappreciated, and it's going to become dramatically more important as AI moves from answering questions to taking actions.

Several delegates mentioned an inability to confidently delete data from production systems, not because they wanted to keep it, but because they weren't certain it was safely stored elsewhere. That uncertainty is driving data sprawl. Organisations are hoarding data they should be retiring because they don't trust their recovery posture. And that swamp of accumulated, unverified data is what their AI systems will have to navigate.

But there's a bigger issue coming. We are moving rapidly into an era of agentic AI, systems that don't just generate answers but take actions. Book the meeting. Process the claim. Update the record. Execute the transaction. When AI is a query interface, bad data produces bad answers. When AI is an agent, bad data produces bad *actions*. At scale. Potentially before anyone realises what's happened.

In that world, the ability to recover, to rewind, restore, and roll back, isn't a backup conversation. It's an AI safety conversation. Resilience is now load-bearing infrastructure for the AI era, not an afterthought.

**Who governs all of this?**

The governance question was perhaps the most human element of the whole discussion. The tension many delegates described, a CEO pushing for AI adoption while a CIO tries to explain why moving without the right foundations is dangerous, is fundamentally a governance failure at the executive level.

AI governance is still an emerging discipline in most organisations. Who decides what data AI agents can access? Who reviews the outputs? Who is accountable when something goes wrong? As one delegate noted, AI needs to be treated as a line item on the risk register. Not a technology project, but an organisational risk that requires the same rigour as any other.

The non-human identity question adds another layer of complexity. As agentic AI proliferates, every employee will have AI agents acting on their behalf, accessing systems, generating outputs, and making decisions. The ratio of non-human to human identities in enterprise environments is already higher than most security teams appreciate, and it's growing fast. The permission structures, the audit trails, the accountability frameworks we built for humans don't automatically extend to the AI acting on their behalf. That gap needs governance. And governance requires trust in the underlying data those agents are working with.

## The Organisation That Had Cracked It

Amid all of this, one delegate stood out.

Their organisation, more than ten thousand people, had achieved something the rest of the table was still working toward. Broad AI adoption. Visibility over what the AI was generating. Continuous evaluation of outputs. Multimodal capability across voice, transcription, and action. A genuine cultural embrace of AI as an enabler rather than a threat.

> And the number that stopped the table was this. They estimate AI is giving their people fifteen minutes back in every hour.

Fifteen minutes in every hour. For an organisation of that size, that's an almost incalculable efficiency gain.

But the thing that struck me most wasn't the number. It was what they said they were doing with that time. Not just moving faster. Not just processing more. **Building more human connection**, more genuine conversations with colleagues and customers, more time for the work that actually requires a person to show up as a person.

There's something quietly profound about the idea that the organisations investing most in artificial intelligence might end up being the most human places to work.

What had they done differently? They had treated data as a strategic asset. Classified it. Scanned it continuously. Established visibility over it. Built trust in it. And then, with that foundation in place, they had moved confidently and aggressively on AI, because they knew what they were building on.

The AI wasn't exceptional. The foundation was.

## The Real Question CIOs Should Be Asking

Most AI conversations in boardrooms and briefings right now start with some version of "Which AI tools should we be deploying?"

That's the wrong starting question. Or at least, it's premature.

The question that almost every delegate around that Sydney lunch table was grappling with, whether they had the language for it or not, was simpler and more fundamental. Do we trust our data enough to build AI on top of it?

For most of them, honestly, the answer right now is no. Not fully. Not yet.

That's not a failure. It's clarity. And the CIOs who are holding the line, pushing back on the executive pressure to ship AI before the foundation is ready, are doing exactly the right thing. Not because AI isn't valuable. The fifteen-minutes-per-hour delegate proves it is. But because AI built on untrustworthy data doesn't just underperform. It erodes confidence, generates risk, and produces the kind of high-profile failures that set the whole enterprise AI agenda back years.

One delegate referenced the Kodak moment, the fear of being left behind by moving too slowly. It's a real risk. But Kodak's actual mistake wasn't moving slowly. It was failing to build on the right foundation when the moment came. The organisations that will define the next decade of enterprise AI won't be the ones who moved fastest. They'll be the ones who moved on solid ground.

It's why Veeam acquired Securiti. Because the organisations that will win the next decade of enterprise AI aren't looking for better models. They're looking for a foundation they can actually trust. By bringing together data security, intelligence, and resilience under one roof, we're building exactly that. The CIOs around that Sydney table already know what they need. We intend to give it to them.
