Half a billion people spent years photographing every park bench and statue in their city, thinking they were playing a game. They were actually building a navigation system for robots.

Niantic just revealed that those 30 billion photos (yes, billion!) Pokemon Go players captured have been turned into a mapping system accurate to within centimeters. And now delivery robots are going to be using it to navigate city streets.

If they pull this off, it might be the most successful bait-and-switch in tech history. Millions of people responded by photographing every park bench, statue, and weird public art installation in their city. From every angle. In every weather condition. For free. Better yet, Niantic got players to pay for the privilege. They added features that rewarded scanning landmarks with in-game items. People competed to photograph random objects. The players thought they were earning rewards. Niantic was building a high fidelity 3D map of the world.

Now those scans are teaching robots how to navigate without GPS, which if you have ever tried to map a run in a dense city you will know doesn't work well due to buildings blocking signals. Coco Robotics in partnership is now using Niantic's "Visual Positioning System" or VPS to let their delivery bots recognise landmarks and know exactly where they are. No satellites required, just the collective obsession of people who wanted that that next Pokemon.

No research lab could have convinced people to map the entire planet for them. But a cute digital monster? Half a billion people *became unpaid cartographers* and didn't even realise it.

Delivery robots will collect even more data as they roll around, feeding it back into the system. The Pokemon trainers built version 1.0, the robots are building version 2.0.

This isn't new. Google's been making you identify traffic lights, buses, bicycles in CAPTCHA tests for years to train their self-driving cars. You thought you were proving you weren't a robot... right?

Niantic's CEO said making Pikachu run around realistically and making delivery robots navigate safely is "actually the same problem." Which is true but also sounds like someone trying not to laugh about how well their plan worked.

**Is This the Future of Datasets?**

Niantic just proved that gamification perhaps beats compensation every time. It is certainly more simple. 

Traditional data collection is expensive and slow. Hire contractors, pay hourly, send them places, hope they're thorough. Niantic made data collection the fun part. They didn't pay people to map the world. 

This is the blueprint now. Every tech company is looking at Pokemon Go thinking "what's our Pikachu?" Want to train AI to recognise dog breeds? Make a game about photographing dogs. Need voice samples in different accents? Build a karaoke app. Want indoor maps? Hide treasure hunts in malls.

No purchased dataset could match the scale and diversity of what Pokemon Go captured. You can't hire someone to photograph a landmark in the rain at 6am from a child's height. But a kid trying to catch a Mewtwo before school? They'll do it unprompted.

Is this fair? Those players created something incredibly valuable that's now powering commercial robotics. They got a digital Pokemon for their collection in return. No revenue sharing, no compensation, just the feeling that maybe you should have read the terms of service.

Then again, people knew they were being tracked. The app required location services. Whether they understood their scans would train robot navigation is different. But when does "I didn't read the fine print" stop being valid?

We're probably one viral app away from crowdsourcing datasets for everything from medical diagnoses to climate modeling. Make it fun enough, and people will generate data that would cost billions to collect traditionally.

This is the future of datasets. The age of paying people to create training data is ending. We are even seeing this with the likes of synthetic datasets for large foundational models being utilised. The age of tricking them into doing it for a dopamine hit is beginning. Dystopian? Maybe. But you're probably downloading the next viral game anyway.

At least the robots will know where they're going and your pizza should be warm when it gets there.
