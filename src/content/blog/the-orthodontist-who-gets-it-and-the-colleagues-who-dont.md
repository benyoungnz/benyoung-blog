This week I took my son to the orthodontist. It was a 20-minute appointment that somehow turned into over an hour. The orthodontist was clearly a technology person. You could see it in the clinic setup, and it came through in how he talked. As I love to do, we went down a tech rabbit hole covering programming, custom software, and a range of other topics before we hit on something that took up the majority of the time.

He mentioned a conference he'd recently attended in Orlando. An orthodontists' conference. One of the sessions was on cybersecurity, run by a someone similar in the industry who had lived through a ransomware attack, with the FBI there to add context on the broader threat landscape.

He said the session was excellent, and he agreed with the central premise that in reality it is not if, but when. What stayed with him, though, was how few of his colleagues showed up for it. The room was almost empty.

These are business owners running clinics, holding patient records, processing payments etc. A week of downtime would hurt most of them badly. And yet when a session came up on one of the more serious threats to their businesses, most of them were somewhere else.

It's not surprising, this pattern is all too common. Cyber risk feels abstract until it isn't. You can't see it or schedule it, and it's easy to assume it's more likely to happen to someone else. Bigger companies, more visible targets, not a dental clinic in the suburbs. There's also the delegation factor that someone in IT is handling it. Which for a lot of small organisations is outsourced to small IT providers or completed in-house by someone with another role. 

So what can we learn from industry data on this?

Businesses with 11 to 1,000 employees account for roughly two-thirds of all ransomware attacks. Ransomware-as-a-service affiliates have industrialised the targeting of smaller organisations. Low cost to attack, high probability of payment, because small businesses often lack the recovery infrastructure to say no. That's from Coveware's Q1 2026 report, not survey data by the way. Coveware handles over 1,000 cyber extortion cases every year, and is now part of Veeam.

Healthcare itself accounts for 17.6% of cases, the highest of any sector, other industry data mirrors or is close to this. Patient records, billing systems, appointment infrastructure. All of it critical, much of it regulated or about to be. A dental clinic can't close for a week. 

The threat has also shifted in ways many haven't registered yet. Exfiltration was the primary leverage mechanism in 73% of cases in Q1 2026. Attackers steal the data first, then decide whether to encrypt. Even with clean backups and a method of recovery, you may still be dealing with a breach notification obligation and the fallout of patient data appearing somewhere it shouldn't. Backups solve downtime. They don't solve data theft.

AI is adding another layer to this. The window between a vulnerability being disclosed and a working exploit existing in the wild is getting shorter. Recent studies with mythos-class models shows AI-assisted discovery finding decade-old flaws in widely-used software that had already passed extensive automated testing for years. Patch cycles that used to give teams days are now measured in hours for the highest-risk systems.

The orthodontist I spoke with came away from that session thinking about what he needed to do differently to protect his business and his patients. He showed up, he listened, and it changed how he thinks about the risk. Most of his colleagues skipped it.

The biggest part of the gap isn't technical. It's that the people making the decisions about budget and risk tolerance aren't engaging with the problem. 

The session was free. It was at a conference they'd already paid to attend. Most of them chose a networking lunch or another session. 

If there's one thing worth taking from this is the next time a session like that comes up (a briefing, a tabletop exercise, a conversation about what happens when your systems go down), go yourself.

I will leave you with this thought. **The empty chairs are the problem**.
